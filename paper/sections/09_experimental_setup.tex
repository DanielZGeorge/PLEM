\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}

\subsection{Synthetic Benchmark}
\label{subsec:synthetic-setup}

The synthetic benchmark is the $128\times128$ scene from \texttt{notebooks/metric\_comparison.ipynb},
a vertical road stripe (class 1) and a $40\times40$ building rectangle (class 2), perturbed by the
nine sweep functions of \texttt{tests/test\_sensitivity.py} listed in Table~\ref{tab:sweeps}.

\subsection{Real-Data Sample}
\label{subsec:realdata-setup}

The real-data sample combines SpaceNet SN2 (buildings, $650\times650$px) and SN3 (roads,
$1300\times1300$px) tiles from the public, unauthenticated S3 bucket. A 1300px road tile spans a
$2\times2$ block of 650px building tiles, matched through an exact index of every building tile's
extent (Section~\ref{subsec:scaled-setup}). The perturbation study uses 50 tiles (25 each from
Vegas and Khartoum, drawn with a fixed seed), plus two Khartoum tiles cached separately as case
studies. For the point class (trees) we added 15 crops of ISPRS Potsdam (6cm/px) \cite{isprs-potsdam} from a
Kaggle mirror, thresholded by palette color and labeled by connected components.

No trained extractor was available for most of this evaluation, so the ``prediction'' is real
ground truth run through the same perturbation families as the synthetic sweeps
(\texttt{notebooks/real\_data\_dtaf1\_topo.ipynb} for roads and buildings,
\texttt{notebooks/real\_data\_evaluation.ipynb} for points) at fixed severities: \texttt{road\_offset}
$\in\{0,4,8,12,16\}$px, \texttt{road\_dropout}$\in\{0,25,50,75\}\%$,
\texttt{road\_thicken}$\in\{0,2,4,6\}$px, \texttt{building\_erode}$\in\{0,1,2,3\}$px,
\texttt{point\_jitter}$\in\{0,3,6,10,15\}$px, \texttt{point\_dropout}$\in\{0,25,50,75,100\}\%$ and
\texttt{point\_clutter}$\in\{0,5,10,15,20\}$ extra blobs.

\subsection{Loss Ablation Setup}
\label{subsec:lossablation-setup}

The loss ablation follows the synthetic-sweep method of Section~\ref{subsec:motivation-setup}
exactly. \texttt{tests/test\_loss\_sensitivity.py} imports its scene builders and perturbation
functions from \texttt{tests/test\_sensitivity.py}, so every loss-side sweep perturbs the same
synthetic scene as its evaluation counterpart. Each sweep records the loss value, not an accuracy,
for a perturbed prediction against the fixed ground truth. The prediction is encoded as
confidently peaked logits (softmax close to one-hot at the perturbed label, at a moderate
confidence scale) and passed through \texttt{PLEMMultiTaskLoss}, and the sweep reports five
configurations built from its sub-terms: \texttt{ce\_dice\_only}, \texttt{plus\_tolerance},
\texttt{plus\_cldice}, \texttt{plus\_heatmap} and \texttt{full}. Seven sweeps mirror seven of the
nine synthetic families: road offset, breakage and thickness, building erosion, and point jitter,
dropout and clutter. \texttt{sweep\_class\_imbalance} and \texttt{sweep\_sparse\_class\_offset}
have no loss-side analog, since they concern how the reductions of the evaluation composites
behave rather than a property of one loss term.

\textbf{Confidence scale.} The loss unit tests (Section~\ref{sec:results},
\texttt{tests/test\_losses\_sanity.py}) use a fully confident logit encoding (softmax
$\approx 1-10^{-10}$) to test behavior under a perfect prediction. The loss-ablation sweeps
deliberately use a more moderate scale (softmax $\approx 0.999$). At full confidence, the
focal-loss log term of \texttt{PointHeatmapLoss} saturates at its numerical clamp for every
confidently wrong pixel, and because each predicted point blob spans several pixels, a handful of
false-positive points can inflate a sweep's total loss by roughly two orders of magnitude, an
artifact of a confidence no real model output reaches. The underlying point does carry over to
real training: the heatmap term is a CornerNet-style focal loss rather than a ratio bounded in
$[0,1]$ like the tolerance and clDice terms, so its magnitude scales differently, which is why
\texttt{PLEMMultiTaskLoss} has a per-term \texttt{weights} dict.

\subsection{Production Training Setup}
\label{subsec:scaled-setup}

No single public dataset annotates all three feature types, so one model is trained on several
sources at once under the per-source class masking of Section~\ref{subsec:multitask-loss}.

\textbf{Model.} A small U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet}, \texttt{SmallUNet}, with a 3-level
encoder/decoder, a base of 16 channels doubling per level and 483,492 parameters. Channels 0--2
form a softmax over background, road and building; channel 3 is an independent sigmoid
intersection head. Tiles are split 70/15/15 at the tile level, separately for each source, so that
adjacent patches cannot leak across the split, and cut into $256\times256$ patches.

\textbf{Development path.} A 2-epoch CPU run on SpaceNet and Potsdam first confirmed that the masked
multi-source loss trains, with all four sub-terms decreasing. The pipeline below then went through
six GPU runs of up to 40 epochs. The point class started as Potsdam trees and cars inside the softmax and
became road intersections on a separate head after run 4; Table~\ref{tab:scaled-history} lists the
failure and fix behind each change.

\textbf{Sources.} SpaceNet (Section~\ref{subsec:realdata-setup}) supplies roads, buildings and,
from its road vectors, intersections. Potsdam supplies buildings only. SpaceNet6 provides Capella
Space SAR imagery over Rotterdam, also with building-footprint labels only. We convert it to a pseudo-RGB image by percentile-clip
normalization, so it passes through the unmodified 3-channel \texttt{SmallUNet}. SAR is an active
sensor and looks the same day or night, which makes it the real, illumination-invariant half of a
two-part approach to night robustness. The synthetic half is a low-light simulation (gamma
darkening, brightness, contrast and saturation jitter, CLAHE and sensor-noise injection) applied to
a random 40\% of training patches from every source. The sample has 1,033 tiles
(550 SpaceNet, 200 Potsdam and 283 SpaceNet6), split 70/15/15 separately for each source into 723
training, 154 validation and 156 test tiles. The sources are not resampled to a common ground
sample distance (${\sim}0.3$--$0.5$\,m/px for SpaceNet against 6\,cm/px for Potsdam), so a Potsdam patch
covers a much smaller area than a SpaceNet patch of the same size (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
The final configuration is
\texttt{SOURCE\_CLASSES = \{"spacenet": [1,2,3], "potsdam": [2], "spacenet6": [2]\}}.

\textbf{Point labels: road intersections.} \texttt{datasets/intersections.py} derives
intersections from the vector road centerlines of SpaceNet~3. It counts road ``arms'' at every
vertex shared between road LineStrings (an endpoint contributes one arm and an interior vertex two)
and keeps vertices with three or more arms. This covers X- and T-junctions, including a T whose
through road is not split at the junction. Lines that cross without a shared vertex, such as
overpasses, are not counted. Nodes closer than 20\,px (6\,m) are merged, since complex junctions and
dual carriageways otherwise produce tight clusters. On real SN3 geojsons this vertex rule agrees
with geometric noding on 11 of 12 sampled tiles, and every derived point falls on a road pixel. The
cached sample holds 8,907 intersections over 550 tiles (16.2 per tile, with 528 tiles holding at
least one). Points are scored on SpaceNet tiles only, with \texttt{point\_f1} at a tolerance of
10\,px (3\,m), the same tolerance roads get, and the headline figure is the micro-averaged F1 over
all test tiles.

\textbf{Building labels and the \texttt{building\_valid} mask.} An exact index of every SN2
building tile's extent, read from the first 64\,KB of each tif, replaces the approximate search of
Section~\ref{subsec:realdata-setup}, and every overlapping building tile is rasterized. Some
$2\times2$ blocks of building tiles are missing from the public SN2 training set, so each tile also
records where building labels are known. Over all 257 Paris road tiles, only 9\% are fully covered,
and on average 55\% of tile area has building labels. Across the 550-tile sample, coverage is 64\%
of tile area, with 80 tiles fully covered and 254 at or below half. Repairing the labels raised the
count of building pixels from 65.7M to 96.6M. Training switches building supervision off for any
SpaceNet patch that touches an unlabelled area (5,941 of 13,860 SpaceNet training patches, or 43\%;
Section~\ref{subsec:multitask-loss}), and evaluation drops predicted buildings in unlabelled areas
before scoring.

\textbf{Training and evaluation.} We train for 40 epochs on a single A100 with batch size 48, mixed
precision, a cosine learning-rate schedule and gradient clipping at norm 5.0, and select the best
checkpoint and stop early (patience 8) on a validation selection loss. The bias of the point head's
output channel starts at $-4.6$ (probability ${\approx}\,0.01$). After training, the point
threshold is chosen by micro F1 on the SpaceNet validation tiles over a grid from 0.002 to 0.5, and
test tiles are never used to choose it. Each tile is scored only on the feature types its source
annotates. A dark-test sweep re-scores every test tile at four fixed low-light severities
(0.0/0.3/0.6/0.9).

\subsection{Conventional-Training Baseline Setup}
\label{subsec:baseline-setup}

To separate what the joint loss contributes from what the pipeline contributes, we train a
baseline in the same notebook with everything held fixed except the loss and the rule for selecting
a checkpoint. The data, tile split, patches, building-coverage masking, per-source class masking
(Section~\ref{subsec:multitask-loss}), low-light augmentation, seed, network, optimizer, schedule,
40-epoch ceiling and patience of 8 are all the same.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Loss.} Cross-entropy plus soft Dice on the road/building softmax, the same function
    as the base term of \texttt{PLEMMultiTaskLoss}, which a parity test confirms is identical. For
    intersections, per-pixel binary cross-entropy plus soft Dice on the sigmoid channel against the
    stamped $5\times5$ intersection squares, which treats intersections as an ordinary rare
    foreground class. The BCE positive weight is the training set's ratio of background to
    intersection pixels, capped at 100 (the raw ratio is 5,770). There is no tolerance, clDice or
    heatmap term, and no low starting bias on the point channel.
  \item \textbf{Selection.} Early stopping and the best checkpoint use validation mIoU over
    background, road, building and intersection, the usual practice in the field, while the joint
    model keeps its validation-loss rule.
  \item \textbf{Intersection decoding.} We threshold the probability map, take connected
    components and report each centroid, the standard path for a binary mask. This is done at the
    conventional 0.5 and at a threshold chosen on validation tiles by the same procedure as for the
    joint model, so a difference in intersection scores cannot come from the choice of threshold
    alone.
  \item \textbf{Scoring.} Both models are scored on the same 156 test tiles with conventional
    metrics and with PLEM's metrics. The conventional metrics are pixel accuracy, per-class IoU
    and mIoU (a class the source never annotates is mapped to background on both sides, and
    buildings are scored where labels exist), and pixel IoU of the intersection squares.
    Differences are paired by tile, and we report the mean, a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval
    (10,000 resamples) and a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
\end{itemize}

The comparison tests two separate claims: that conventional and PLEM metrics can disagree about
which of two models is better (the \emph{metric claim}), and that the joint loss is better on
PLEM's metrics when training is matched (the \emph{loss claim}).
