\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{IoU-family segmentation metrics}
Region-overlap IoU, the baseline this paper argues against for linear features, is the standard
evaluation convention on general-purpose semantic-segmentation benchmarks such as PASCAL VOC
\cite{everingham2010pascalvoc} and Cityscapes \cite{cordts2016cityscapes}. Neither benchmark
contains a thin, 1-pixel-wide linear class, so their leaderboard practice never had to deal with
IoU's sensitivity to road width. The ISPRS Potsdam benchmark
\cite{rottensteiner2012isprs,rottensteiner2014isprs} does include a building class close in kind
to PLEM's polygon classes, and modern models there report mean IoU up to ${\sim}86\%$ and mean F1
up to ${\sim}92\%$, so plain region-overlap metrics are already close to saturated on well-behaved
polygon classes, where a high score can still hide a failure mode. The road numbers in
Section~\ref{subsec:related-roads} show this more starkly.

\subsection{Topology-/skeleton-aware metrics for linear structures}
clDice \cite{shit2021cldice} and related centerline methods compare the skeletons of the predicted
and ground-truth masks instead of their full areas, so they score the extracted centerline
independently of how wide the structure is drawn.

\subsection{Boundary-based metrics for polygonal features}
The boundary F1 (BF) score \cite{csurka2013evaluation} matches boundary pixels of the predicted and
ground-truth regions within a distance tolerance, so it rewards an accurate outline rather than
overlapping area.

\subsection{Road-network extraction and connectivity metrics}
\label{subsec:related-roads}
APLS, the metric of the SpaceNet road-extraction challenge \cite{vanetten2018spacenet}, and TOPO
\cite{biagioni2012topo} are the two dominant graph-connectivity metrics for road networks, and they
motivate the \texttt{apls} and \texttt{dtaf1\_topo} primitives of Sections~\ref{subsec:apls}
and~\ref{sec:topological}. Published road-extraction leaderboards plateau well short of 1.0 even at
their best. The winning submission to SpaceNet Challenge 3 scored APLS 0.666, with the field
topping out at ${\approx}0.67$; CRESI, the best-known raster-to-graph pipeline, reaches APLS
$0.69 \pm 0.02$ \cite{vanetten2019cresi}; and D-LinkNet, winner of the DeepGlobe 2018 Road
Extraction Challenge, reports pixel IoU 0.647 on validation and 0.634 on test
\cite{zhou2018dlinknet}, a pixel-only figure that says nothing about whether the extracted network
stays connected. SpaceNet's building-footprint challenges show the same pattern from the other
direction: the first SpaceNet building challenge topped out at F1 0.26, and the winner of the
four-city SpaceNet~2 challenge reached F1 0.693 averaged across cities (both from
\cite{vanetten2018spacenet} and the associated challenge results). These numbers are context only:
we evaluate a small curated subset of tiles under our own protocol, not the official SpaceNet,
DeepGlobe or Potsdam test sets, so the two are not directly comparable. They do show that the best
published pixel and topology scores sit between 0.26 and 0.93, and a single scalar in that range
cannot tell scattered pixel noise within tolerance apart from a fragmented network.
\texttt{dtaf1\_topo} and \texttt{apls} close this gap: on the road-breakage sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:topological}, \texttt{dtaf1} stays at 1.0 through 70\% dropout while
\texttt{dtaf1\_topo} and \texttt{apls} collapse.

\subsection{Point/instance detection metrics}
Point detection is usually scored with COCO-style average precision \cite{lin2014coco}, and
\texttt{point\_f1}'s Hungarian assignment draws on the optimal bipartite-matching literature
\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}. Road intersections, PLEM's point feature, are the vertices of the road
graph, which graph-based road extraction predicts explicitly, either by iterative graph
construction (RoadTracer \cite{bastani2018roadtracer}) or by graph-tensor encoding (Sat2Graph
\cite{he2020sat2graph}). PLEM instead predicts them as a separate point output next to a raster road
mask and scores them as instances.

\subsection{Composite/multi-task segmentation evaluation}
Existing work evaluates linear and polygonal feature types with separate metrics whose scores
cannot be compared. The evaluation half of PLEM fills this gap with one formula for every class
(DTAF1) and a deliberately harsh cross-type composite (CBHM).

\subsection{Differentiable segmentation losses}
\label{subsec:related-losses}
The same gap exists on the training side. Standard multiclass segmentation losses, cross-entropy
and soft Dice \cite{milletari2016vnet}, ignore geometry in the same way Section~\ref{sec:intro}
argues IoU does: they have no notion of positional tolerance, centerline topology or
point-instance identity. Three earlier lines of work each address one of these properties in
isolation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Boundary and Hausdorff-distance losses} precompute a distance transform on the
    ground truth and use it as a fixed per-pixel weight against the model's soft output
    \cite{kervadec2019boundary,karimi2019hausdorff}. They are the direct precedent for the soft
    tolerance-band term of Section~\ref{subsec:tolerance-loss}.
  \item \textbf{Soft-clDice} \cite{shit2021cldice}, from the same paper as the clDice metric, is a
    differentiable soft-skeletonization loss for tubular structures and the direct precedent for
    Section~\ref{subsec:cldice-loss}.
  \item \textbf{Heatmap regression for keypoint detection} recasts discrete point detection as
    dense regression against Gaussian targets instead of instance matching, as in CornerNet
    \cite{law2018cornernet} and CenterNet \cite{zhou2019centernet}. It is the direct precedent for
    Section~\ref{subsec:heatmap-loss}.
\end{enumerate}
None of these combines the three properties. Section~\ref{sec:method} combines relaxations of
(1)--(3) into one multi-task loss that covers all three at once, together with a masked
multi-source training scheme (Section~\ref{subsec:multitask-loss}) that lets datasets annotating
different subsets of the feature types jointly supervise one model.
